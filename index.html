Lapping

← All projects

Two people race round a track, and one is faster than the other. How long until the faster one laps the slower one? The answer is one short equation, and the same equation tells you when the hands of a clock meet, why the Moon's phases repeat every 29½ days and not every 27⅓, and how often the Earth overtakes Mars.

Z = (X × Y) / (X − Y)

X is the slower runner's lap time, Y the faster runner's, and Z the time between one passing and the next.

Michael Carnright worked this equation out for himself at sixteen. It is the same relation astronomers use for a synodic period, the time for two orbiting bodies to come back into line, though he arrived at it from two runners on a track. The page and its code are by Claude Code.

Why it works

Measure speed in laps per unit of time. The faster runner covers 1/Y of a lap each unit of time and the slower one 1/X, so the faster runner pulls ahead by 1/Y − 1/X of a lap each unit. It passes the slower runner again when it has pulled ahead by one whole lap:

Z × (1/Y − 1/X) = 1

and solving for Z gives Z = 1 / (1/Y − 1/X) = XY / (X − Y). In that time the faster runner has run Z/Y = X/(X − Y) laps and the slower one Z/X = Y/(X − Y), exactly one fewer.

Nothing in this needs the runners to start together, or the track to be any particular length. Only the two lap times matter. That is why the same equation fits a clock face and the solar system: anything that goes round and round at a steady rate is a runner on a track.

  • If X is barely more than Y, X − Y is tiny and Z is huge: two runners of almost equal speed run side by side for a long time.
  • If X is much larger than Y, Z is only a little more than Y: the slower runner is nearly standing still, and the faster one has to cover just a bit more than one lap to catch it.
  • If X = 2Y, Z = 2Y = X: the faster runner catches up once each time the slower one completes a lap.
